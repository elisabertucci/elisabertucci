Every studio project I've worked on has hit the same wall: the right material exists, but the info about it is scattered across supplier PDFs, scribbled notes, and half-remembered workshop tips. Nothing a whole team can search or compare together.

So I spent my BA thesis year building the fix: Material.Data, a digital materials library for University of Genoa's design, architecture, and engineering students — cataloguing properties, sustainability profile, and real-world use in one place.

It actually started as environmental research, not a design brief. Deep in life-cycle assessment and waste-based biomaterials, one thing kept clicking: as bio-based and new materials multiply, picking the right one only gets harder without a guide.

Before designing a single screen, I did the unglamorous part: benchmarked 6 existing platforms — Material ConneXion, Materiom, MaterialDistrict, Brain of Materials, Material Bank, and the Granta EduPack tool already used in my own materials course.

Then I synthesized all six into one composite user-flow before sketching my own. Good classification isn't invented from a blank page — it's assembled from what already works, and fixed where it doesn't.

Next: 10 open-ended interviews around Genoa — architecture, design, and engineering (mechanical, civil/environmental) students, a biotech student, a professor, a course coordinator. Open questions on purpose, to catch real workflows, not polished answers.

My results contained: Outdated library loans, a single Youtuber channel to trust, LLM's and forum threads, and a friend's carpentry shop, for the one material the university couldn't offer.

The sharpest pattern: an architect and a structural engineer can look at the same material and want opposite things from it — one calculating load, one judging how it reads in a render. Same material, two different jobs.

That reframed the brief: not "list materials," but architect ONE classification that serves both — engineering-grade filters next to cost and application filters, cross-linked into separate Design and Architecture views off the same material.

The moment it clicked: a couple of interviewees had never heard of bio-based materials — until I described them, then they were hooked. So the datasheet doesn't just describe a material — where data allows, it surfaces similar ones too, some possibly more sustainable.

That's the loop closing: an environmental-research thesis that ends up nudging every search toward a better option, without lecturing anyone about it.

I stress-tested the direction against the department's own history: DAD ran a physical materioteca from 2006–2015, shut down over space and staffing costs. Asked directly, the department director's read was clear: digital-first is the right call now.
